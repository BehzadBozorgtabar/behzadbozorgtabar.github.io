About

Adaptive AI for a changing world

My research asks how learning systems keep performing when the world drifts away from their training data. With my group at Aarhus University, we develop test-time adaptation methods that update models on unlabelled streams, self-supervised representations that transfer across domains and tasks, and uncertainty estimates that tell a model when it should not be trusted. Much of this work is tested in medical imaging, where silent errors are costly.

A growing part of my research takes these ideas into the physical world. In the STORM project, funded by the Independent Research Fund Denmark and starting in 2027, we will study how robots can use touch to notice unexpected physical changes, remember contact, and correct or stop when uncertainty is too high. With vision–language models that reason over images and text, this points towards agents that observe, adapt and act safely in changing real-world conditions.

Before joining Aarhus University in 2026, I was Head of the Computer Vision Team and Lecturer at École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL) from 2017 to 2025, with a joint position as Senior Researcher in the Department of Radiology at Lausanne University Hospital (CHUV) from 2019 to 2025, and earlier a Postdoctoral Research Fellow at IBM Research Australia. I received my PhD from the University of Canberra in 2016. I am also a Guest Professor at EPFL.

I am an elected Scholar of the European Laboratory for Learning and Intelligent Systems (ELLIS) and Advisor in the ELLIS PhD & Postdoc Programme, affiliated with the ELLIS Unit Lausanne at EPFL, where I support joint and co-supervised PhD positions across the ELLIS network.

Adaptive AI and test-time learning

How can a deployed model keep learning from unlabelled test data when the world drifts, recurs and never stands still?

Models are trained once and then meet conditions that keep moving. This theme develops methods that adapt at deployment time, without source data or labels. The work includes self-training with learned adversarial augmentations, learnable test-time augmentation for medical image segmentation, normalisation statistics that stay correct when test streams are temporally correlated, and a reservoir of specialised models that recognises recurring domains instead of relearning them. Across these projects the question is how adaptation can stay stable over long, evolving deployments.

Reliable multimodal and medical AI

When a vision–language model reads a scan or answers a clinical question, how do we know when to trust it?

Foundation models now read radiology images, answer medical questions and ground language in anatomy, yet confident errors and hallucinated findings remain. This theme builds multimodal medical AI whose uncertainty is explicit. It includes transductive conformal prediction that returns set-valued answers with coverage guarantees for medical vision–language models, counterfactual decoding that suppresses hallucinations in medical visual question answering, disease-aware vision–language pretraining for 3D CT, and prompt learning with vision–language models for few-shot histopathology.

Self-supervised representation learning

What can a model learn about objects, scenes and tissue from unlabelled images alone, and can it tell when something is out of place?

Labels are scarce and expensive, especially in medicine, so this theme learns visual representations without them. Dense and object-level self-supervision, through cross-view online clustering and cross-image object-level bootstrapping, yields features that transfer to segmentation and detection. A unified encoder brings image and video understanding together in one model. The same ideas support anomaly and out-of-distribution detection, where a model learns what normal chest X-rays or tissue types look like and flags whatever departs from it.

Physical AI and world models

Can a robot use touch to notice when something unexpected happens, remember it, and correct or stop before harm is done?

This is the newest direction of the lab and extends adaptive AI from images to physical interaction. Robotic foundation models act on vision and language, but they rarely sense what happens at the point of contact. STORM, funded by the Independent Research Fund Denmark and starting in March 2027 together with the University of Southern Denmark, will combine soft, fluid-filled tactile skin with adaptive AI so that robots sense and remember contact, recognise risks such as slipping objects, and correct their movements or stop when uncertainty is too high. In the longer term, the aim is agents that anticipate the consequences of their own actions before taking them.

Adaptive & Agentic AI (A3) Lab

I founded and direct the A3 Lab at Aarhus University. We build trustworthy adaptive and agentic AI, with a focus on test-time adaptation, multimodal foundation models and self-supervised learning, for safe and auditable deployment under domain shift in real-world settings.
